## What Is Your Inner Child?

Your inner child is the part of you that still carries the emotions, beliefs, and wounds from childhood. When your adult self overreacts, shuts down, or feels small — your inner child is often the one responding.

## Signs Your Inner Child Needs Attention
- Disproportionate emotional reactions to minor events
- Fear of abandonment or rejection
- Difficulty trusting people
- People-pleasing or excessive need for approval
- Harsh self-criticism (the voice of a critical parent, internalised)
- Difficulty playing, being spontaneous, or having fun
- Shame about normal human needs (rest, attention, comfort)
- Feeling "not enough" regardless of achievement

## The Reparenting Process

### Step 1: Acknowledge
"I have an inner child who was hurt. That child's feelings are valid."

### Step 2: Listen
When strong emotions arise, ask: "How old do I feel right now? What does this little version of me need?"

### Step 3: Validate
Say to your inner child: "I see you. I hear you. What happened wasn't okay. You didn't deserve that."

### Step 4: Provide What Was Missing
- If you were criticised → Give yourself praise
- If you were neglected → Give yourself attention
- If you were controlled → Give yourself choices
- If you were abandoned → Commit to never leaving yourself
- If you were told to be perfect → Give yourself permission to be messy

### Step 5: Create Safety
"I am the adult now. I will protect you. You are safe."

## Inner Child Exercises

### Letter Writing
Write a letter TO your childhood self. What do they need to hear?
Write a letter FROM your childhood self. What do they want to tell you?

### Photo Work
Find a childhood photo. Look at that child with compassion. What would you say to them?

### Play
Do something your inner child enjoyed:
- Colour, draw, paint
- Dance without caring how it looks
- Build something with your hands
- Watch your favourite childhood movie
- Eat your childhood comfort food

## Inner Child Wounds in Indian Families
- Emotional neglect disguised as "we provided everything" (materially but not emotionally)
- Parentification: being forced to be the responsible child
- Comparison with siblings ("Why can't you be more like...")
- Physical discipline normalised ("We turned out fine")
- Gender-based restrictions or favouritism
- Being silenced: "Don't talk back" / "Children should be seen, not heard"
